Tenses

A tense tells when an action happens and whether it is finished. Tense errors are the most common blank in sentence completion, so master the twelve forms and the signal words that go with them.

Start from zero

The twelve tenses

PastPresentFuture
SimpleI workedI workI will work
ContinuousI was workingI am workingI will be working
PerfectI had workedI have workedI will have worked
Perfect continuousI had been workingI have been workingI will have been working

When to use each

Simple present for habits and facts (She goes to school; The sun rises in the east). Present continuous for actions happening now. Present perfect for past actions connected to now (I have finished). Simple past for finished actions at a known time (I finished yesterday). Past perfect for the earlier of two past actions (The train had left when we arrived). Future perfect for an action finished before a future time (By June I will have completed the course).
Tenses on a timeline

Signal words

every day, usually, always → simple present. now, at the moment → present continuous. since, for, already, yet, just, ever → present perfect. yesterday, ago, last week → simple past. by the time, before, after (two past events) → past perfect. by next month, by then → future perfect.

Conditionals and reported speech

If + simple present → will (If it rains, we will stay). If + simple past → would (If I were you, I would apply). If + past perfect → would have (If I had left earlier, I would have caught it). In reported speech, the tense usually moves one step back (He said that he was tired). Facts that are always true keep the present tense.

Formula and rule box

  • since + point in time; for + duration
  • by the time + past perfect (earlier) and past (later)
  • If + present → will; If + past → would; If + past perfect → would have
  • I wish + past (present wish); I wish + past perfect (past wish)
  • Reported speech: present → past, past → past perfect
  • Universal truths stay in the simple present

Step-by-step method

  1. Find the time words in the sentence.
  2. Decide: habit, now, finished, or connected to now?
  3. Choose the form from the table.
  4. Check it against nearby verbs for consistency.

Solved examples

Example 1. She ___ (go) to school every day.
Solution: 'Every day' signals a habit, so simple present: she goes.
Answer: goes
Example 2. By the time we reached, the train ___ (leave).
Solution: Two past events; the train left first, so past perfect: had left.
Answer: had left
Example 3. I ___ (live) here since 2020.
Solution: 'Since' needs present perfect: have lived (or have been living).
Answer: have lived
Example 4. If it rains tomorrow, we ___ (stay) at home.
Solution: First conditional: if + present → will.
Answer: will stay

Shortcuts

  • since/for → present perfect; yesterday/ago → simple past.
  • In 'by the time' sentences use past perfect for the earlier event.
  • After 'wish' use a past form.

Common mistakes

  • Using simple past with 'since'.
  • Mixing two time frames in one sentence.
  • Using 'will' after 'if' in the condition part.

Subject–Verb Agreement

A verb must match its subject in number. The trick is to find the real subject and ignore the words that sit between the subject and the verb.

Start from zero

The basic rule

A singular subject takes a singular verb (The boy runs) and a plural subject takes a plural verb (The boys run). Find the true subject first. In 'The list of items is long', the subject is 'list', not 'items'.

Always singular

each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, everybody, nobody, somebody, anyone: Each of the boys has a book. One of my friends is a doctor. Uncountable nouns (information, furniture, advice, news, bread) and subjects like mathematics, physics, news also take singular verbs.
A flow for subject–verb agreement

Joined subjects

With and the subject is plural (Ram and Shyam are friends) unless the two form one idea (Bread and butter is my breakfast). With either…or, neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject (Neither the manager nor the employees were present). With along with, as well as, together with, the verb agrees with the first subject (The captain, along with his team, is arriving).

Collective nouns and special phrases

A collective noun (team, committee, family) is singular when acting as one unit (The team is winning) and plural when members act separately (The committee are divided in their opinions). The number of is singular; a number of is plural.

Fractions and quantities

Two-thirds of the work is done; two-thirds of the students are present (the noun after 'of' decides). A sum of money is singular: Fifty rupees is enough.

Formula and rule box

  • each / every / either / neither → singular
  • The number of → singular; A number of → plural
  • X along with Y → verb agrees with X
  • Either X or Y / Neither X nor Y → verb agrees with Y
  • Uncountable nouns (information, advice, news) → singular
  • Subjects of one unit (bread and butter) → singular

Step-by-step method

  1. Underline the subject and cross out phrases in between.
  2. Decide singular or plural.
  3. Check special words (each, number of, along with).
  4. Choose the matching verb.

Solved examples

Example 1. The list of items ___ (is/are) long.
Solution: The subject is 'list' (singular), so 'is'.
Answer: is
Example 2. Neither the manager nor the employees ___ (was/were) present.
Solution: The nearer subject 'employees' is plural, so 'were'.
Answer: were
Example 3. One of my friends ___ (is/are) a doctor.
Solution: 'One' is the subject, so 'is'.
Answer: is
Example 4. A number of students ___ (is/are) absent.
Solution: 'A number of' is plural.
Answer: are

Shortcuts

  • Mentally delete phrases between commas before choosing the verb.
  • 'The number of' singular, 'a number of' plural.
  • For either/or and neither/nor, look at the closest noun.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the verb to match the nearest noun in 'list of items'.
  • Treating 'each of the boys' as plural.
  • Using plural with uncountable nouns like 'news' or 'advice'.

Articles

A, an and the are small words that appear in almost every sentence. The rules are short and the same few patterns repeat in exams.

Start from zero

A and an

Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound. It depends on the sound, not the spelling: an hour (silent h), an MBA (em), a university (yoo), a one-rupee note (won). Use them with singular countable nouns when you mean 'one of many' or mention something for the first time.

The

Use the when the noun is specific or known to both speaker and listener: the book on the table, the sun, the moon. Also use it with superlatives (the best), rivers, seas, mountain ranges and groups of islands (the Ganga, the Himalayas), musical instruments (plays the guitar) and for something already mentioned (I saw a dog. The dog was big).
Choosing the right article

No article

Do not use an article with plural or uncountable nouns in a general sense (Dogs are loyal; Water is essential), with most names of people, cities and countries, with meals (have lunch), languages and sports (plays cricket), and in fixed phrases (go to school, go to bed).

Common traps

Use 'the' before ordinal numbers and 'the same'. Do not use 'the' before 'most' meaning 'many' (Most people agree). 'What a beautiful day!' needs 'a' because it is exclamatory with a singular noun.

Formula and rule box

  • a / an → first mention, singular, one of many
  • the → specific, unique, superlative, rivers, ranges
  • Zero article → plural/general, sports, meals, languages
  • an before vowel sounds: an hour, an MBA
  • a before consonant sounds: a university, a European

Step-by-step method

  1. Is the noun specific or unique? Use 'the'.
  2. Otherwise, is it singular countable? Use a/an by sound.
  3. Plural or uncountable in general: no article.

Solved examples

Example 1. ___ honest man was rewarded.
Solution: 'Honest' starts with a vowel sound (silent h).
Answer: An
Example 2. ___ university is at the end of the road.
Solution: 'University' starts with a 'yoo' sound (consonant sound).
Answer: A
Example 3. He plays ___ guitar.
Solution: Musical instruments take 'the'.
Answer: the
Example 4. ___ Ganga is a holy river.
Solution: Rivers take 'the'.
Answer: The

Shortcuts

  • Say the word aloud: the sound decides a or an.
  • Rivers, seas, ranges and superlatives take 'the'.
  • No article before sports, meals and languages.

Common mistakes

  • Using 'an' before 'university' or 'European'.
  • Adding 'the' before names of countries and cities.
  • Omitting 'the' with superlatives.

Prepositions

Prepositions link nouns to the rest of the sentence. Most are learnt as fixed pairs, so a short list of common combinations wins most marks.

Start from zero

Prepositions of time

At for exact points (at 5 pm, at night, at noon). On for days and dates (on Monday, on 5 May). In for longer periods (in May, in 2024, in the morning, in summer). For shows duration (for two hours). Since shows the starting point (since 2015). By means 'not later than' (by Friday).

Prepositions of place

At for a point (at the bus stop), on for a surface (on the table), in for an enclosed area (in the room). Between two things, among three or more. Above/over, below/under, beside, behind.
Prepositions of time and place

Fixed combinations

good at, bad at, interested in, afraid of, fond of, proud of, depend on, rely on, believe in, different from, similar to, senior/junior to, superior/inferior to, prefer … to, married to, angry with a person, angry at a thing, apologise for, succeed in, insist on, suffer from, aware of.

Phrasal prepositions

in front of, in spite of, on behalf of, because of, according to, instead of, due to, apart from. These behave like a single preposition.

Formula and rule box

  • at + clock time; on + day/date; in + month/year
  • for + duration; since + starting point
  • between (2) / among (3+)
  • prefer X to Y (not 'than')
  • senior/junior/superior/inferior + to
  • different from; similar to

Step-by-step method

  1. Check whether the word is part of a fixed pair.
  2. For time words use the at / on / in ladder.
  3. For place use point, surface, enclosed.
  4. Read the sentence aloud; the correct preposition usually sounds natural.

Solved examples

Example 1. The meeting is ___ 5 pm.
Solution: Exact clock time takes 'at'.
Answer: at
Example 2. She was born ___ 1999.
Solution: A year takes 'in'.
Answer: in
Example 3. He is good ___ mathematics.
Solution: good at.
Answer: at
Example 4. I have waited ___ two hours.
Solution: Duration takes 'for'.
Answer: for

Shortcuts

  • at (point) < on (day) < in (period).
  • prefer ... to ..., never prefer ... than.
  • 'Between' two, 'among' more than two.

Common mistakes

  • Using 'since' with a duration and 'for' with a date.
  • Saying 'senior than' instead of 'senior to'.
  • Mixing 'angry with' and 'angry at'.

Pronouns and Conjunctions

Pronouns replace nouns and conjunctions join ideas. Questions test the correct case of the pronoun and the right connecting word for the logic of the sentence.

Start from zero

Pronoun cases

Use the subject form (I, he, she, we, they) before a verb and the object form (me, him, her, us, them) after a verb or preposition. 'He and I went' (subject), 'Between you and me' (object). After 'than' and 'as', complete the sentence in your head: 'He is taller than I (am)'.

Who, whom, which, whose

Who is the subject (the man who called), whom the object (the man whom I met), whose shows possession and which refers to things. Its is possessive; it's means 'it is'.
Conjunctions grouped by job

Coordinating conjunctions

The seven FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. They join equal parts. 'He worked hard, yet he failed.'

Subordinating conjunctions

because, since, although, though, while, if, unless, until, whereas. 'Although it was raining, we played.' Do not use 'but' with 'although' in the same clause.

Correlative pairs

either … or, neither … nor, both … and, not only … but also, whether … or. The parts after each word must be parallel in form. 'Not only the teacher but also the students were present.'

Meaning of connectors

Contrast: but, yet, however, although, despite. Addition: and, also, moreover. Reason: because, since. Result: so, therefore, hence. Condition: if, unless, provided that. Choice: or, otherwise.

Formula and rule box

  • Subject form before verb; object form after verb/preposition
  • than / as + subject form if the verb is implied
  • who (subject), whom (object), whose (possession)
  • its = belonging to it; it's = it is
  • either … or; neither … nor; both … and; not only … but also
  • although/though ≠ but
  • unless = if … not

Step-by-step method

  1. Decide the role of the word: subject or object.
  2. For conjunctions, identify the relationship: contrast, reason, result, condition.
  3. Pick the connector that matches the logic.
  4. Check pairs: either-or, neither-nor.

Solved examples

Example 1. He and ___ went to the market.
Solution: Subject position: 'I'.
Answer: I
Example 2. Between you and ___, this is a secret.
Solution: After a preposition: object form 'me'.
Answer: me
Example 3. ___ the rain, we went out.
Solution: Contrast with a noun: 'Despite'.
Answer: Despite
Example 4. He worked hard, ___ he failed.
Solution: Contrast: 'yet' (or 'but').
Answer: yet

Shortcuts

  • 'Between you and me' always.
  • Unless = if not; don't add another negative.
  • Match the connector to the relationship before looking at options.

Common mistakes

  • Using 'I' after prepositions.
  • Using 'although … but' together.
  • Confusing 'its' and 'it's'.

Vocabulary and Word Choice

Vocabulary questions test whether you know a word's meaning and can pick the best word for the context. Reading context clues is as important as memorising words.

Start from zero

Synonyms and antonyms

A synonym has nearly the same meaning (diligent = hardworking). An antonym has the opposite meaning (scarce ↔ abundant). Words have shades, so always check them in the sentence.

Context clues

The rest of the sentence often gives the meaning: 'The speaker was eloquent, so the audience listened with interest.' The clue 'listened with interest' suggests a positive word such as fluent.
A word cluster for 'happy'

Commonly confused words

affect (verb: to influence) / effect (noun: result); principal (head, main) / principle (rule); complement (completes) / compliment (praise); stationary (not moving) / stationery (paper items); advice (noun) / advise (verb); accept (receive) / except (excluding); lose / loose; its / it's.

Collocations

Some words go together naturally: make a decision, take a decision (BrE: take), do homework, heavy rain, strong tea, commit a crime, pay attention. Choose the verb that normally goes with the noun.

Word-learning plan

Learn 10 words a day in clusters with a sentence for each. Revise at 1, 3 and 7 days. See the word bank in the extra pages for 40 high-frequency words.

Formula and rule box

  • Find the clue word in the sentence
  • Positive clue → positive word; negative clue → negative word
  • Use the root and prefix: un-, in-, dis- often mean not
  • Eliminate options with the wrong tone (positive/negative)
  • affect = verb, effect = noun (most of the time)

Step-by-step method

  1. Read the sentence and note the tone.
  2. Predict a word before looking at options.
  3. Eliminate options that clash in meaning or tone.
  4. Pick the closest match.

Solved examples

Example 1. Find a synonym of 'diligent'.
Solution: Diligent means hardworking.
Answer: hardworking
Example 2. Find an antonym of 'scarce'.
Solution: Scarce means rare; the opposite is abundant.
Answer: abundant
Example 3. The medicine had a positive ___ on his health.
Solution: Result of an action is a noun: effect.
Answer: effect
Example 4. The ___ of the school addressed the students.
Solution: The head of a school is the principal.
Answer: principal

Shortcuts

  • Predict the answer first and then match the options.
  • Learn words in synonym pairs: lucid–clear, frugal–thrifty.
  • Remember affect (verb) and effect (noun).

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a word with the right meaning but wrong tone.
  • Mixing up principal and principle.
  • Ignoring the clue words in the sentence.

Idioms and Phrasal Verbs

Idioms and phrasal verbs add colour to English. Their meanings are fixed and must be learnt as whole phrases.

Start from zero

Idioms

An idiom's meaning is different from the meaning of its words. 'Break the ice' does not involve ice; it means to start a conversation. Learn the common ones with their meaning and a sample sentence.

Common idioms

a piece of cake (very easy); break the ice (start a conversation); hit the nail on the head (say exactly the right thing); once in a blue moon (very rarely); under the weather (unwell); the ball is in your court (it is your decision); cost an arm and a leg (very expensive); burn the midnight oil (work late); call it a day (stop for the day); spill the beans (reveal a secret); bite the bullet (face something unpleasant); let the cat out of the bag (reveal a secret by accident).
Idioms and their meanings

Phrasal verbs

A verb plus a particle gives a new meaning: give up (quit), look after (take care of), put off (postpone), carry out (perform), turn down (reject), set up (establish), run out of (exhaust), bring up (raise or mention), come across (find by chance), look into (investigate), turn up (arrive), break down (stop working), call off (cancel), take over (assume control).

How to use them

In sentence completion, decide the meaning you need first (postpone, reject, cancel) and then choose the matching phrasal verb. Watch the particle: turn down = reject, turn up = arrive, turn off = switch off.

Formula and rule box

  • put off = postpone
  • call off = cancel
  • turn down = reject
  • turn up = arrive
  • look after = take care of
  • carry out = perform
  • give up = quit
  • break down = stop working

Step-by-step method

  1. Decide the meaning the sentence needs.
  2. Match it to the idiom or phrasal verb.
  3. Check the particle (up, down, off, out).
  4. Ensure the grammar form (tense) still fits.

Solved examples

Example 1. What does 'break the ice' mean?
Solution: It means to start a conversation in an awkward situation.
Answer: start a conversation
Example 2. What does 'give up' mean in 'He gave up smoking'?
Solution: Give up means to quit or stop.
Answer: quit
Example 3. The meeting was ___ because of rain (put off/put on).
Solution: Put off means postponed.
Answer: put off
Example 4. What does 'once in a blue moon' mean?
Solution: It means very rarely.
Answer: very rarely

Shortcuts

  • Learn 5 idioms a day with one sentence each.
  • Group phrasal verbs by particle (turn up / down / off).
  • Guess the meaning from context first.

Common mistakes

  • Translating idioms literally.
  • Mixing the particles of phrasal verbs.
  • Using the wrong tense with phrasal verbs.

Error Spotting and Sentence Structure

Many sentence-completion items are error-correction questions in disguise. A fixed scan of eight things catches almost every mistake.

Start from zero

The eight-point scan

1. Subject–verb agreement. 2. Tense. 3. Article. 4. Preposition. 5. Pronoun. 6. Comparison. 7. Word order. 8. Parallelism. Check them in this order; most errors are in the first four.

Common error patterns

He don't know (doesn't). She is more smarter (smarter: avoid double comparative). The news are good (news is). He suggested me to apply (suggested that I apply). I prefer tea than coffee (prefer tea to coffee). One of my friend is a doctor (friends). He is senior than me (senior to). The furnitures are new (furniture is).
The eight-point error scan

Parallelism

Items in a list should have the same form. Wrong: She likes singing, dancing and to swim. Right: She likes singing, dancing and swimming. Wrong: To read is better than writing. Right: Reading is better than writing.

Redundancy and word order

Avoid repeating the meaning: 'return back', 'repeat again', 'free gift' (just return, repeat, gift). Keep adverbs close to the verb and place 'only' next to the word it modifies.

Reading strategy

Read the sentence once, listening for what sounds wrong. Then test the eight points. Replace the suspect word with the alternative and check if the sentence works.

Formula and rule box

  • Scan order: SV, tense, article, preposition, pronoun, comparison, order, parallelism
  • No double comparative: smarter, not more smarter
  • Uncountable nouns: furniture, information, advice, news → singular, no plural form
  • prefer … to; senior/junior to
  • Lists must be parallel in form

Step-by-step method

  1. Read the sentence and note anything that sounds odd.
  2. Run the eight-point scan.
  3. Fix only the one mistake.
  4. Re-read the corrected sentence.

Solved examples

Example 1. Correct: He don't know the answer.
Solution: Subject 'he' is singular, so 'doesn't'.
Answer: He doesn't know the answer.
Example 2. Correct: She is more smarter than me.
Solution: Double comparative: remove 'more'.
Answer: She is smarter than me.
Example 3. Correct: The news are good.
Solution: 'News' is uncountable and singular.
Answer: The news is good.
Example 4. Correct: She likes singing, dancing and to swim.
Solution: Make the list parallel.
Answer: She likes singing, dancing and swimming.

Shortcuts

  • Read the sentence aloud in your head to catch awkward spots.
  • Check the verb first: most errors involve it.
  • If the sentence has a list, check parallelism.

Common mistakes

  • Changing a correct part of the sentence.
  • Missing double comparatives.
  • Treating uncountable nouns as plural.

Sentence Completion: the 25-second method

In the 2027 pattern there are 20 sentence-completion items with only 25 seconds each. Speed comes from a fixed method, not from reading faster.

Start from zero

What is tested

Each item gives a sentence with a blank and four options. The blank tests grammar (tense, agreement, articles, prepositions), vocabulary (word choice, idioms, collocations) or logic (connectors). Identify the type in the first five seconds.

The four-step routine

1. Read the sentence once and look at the words around the blank. 2. Decide: grammar or vocabulary? 3. Eliminate options that are clearly wrong (wrong tense, wrong preposition, wrong tone). 4. Pick the best option and move on. Never go back for a second look unless time remains.
How to spend 25 seconds

Smart elimination

Check the verb form first. Check the preposition next. Then check the connector's logic. If two options remain, choose the one that sounds natural in context.

Time and accuracy

20 items × 25 s = 500 s (about 8 min 20 s). Do not spend more than 25 seconds; unanswered items score nothing, so guess when unsure. Practise with a timer until the routine is automatic.

Formula and rule box

  • 25 s per item
  • Type first: grammar or vocabulary?
  • Check order: verb form → preposition → connector → word choice
  • Guess when unsure (no negative marking)
  • Do not re-read the sentence more than twice

Step-by-step method

  1. Read once and spot the blank type.
  2. Eliminate wrong options.
  3. Choose and move on within 25 seconds.
  4. Review the mistakes after each timed set.

Solved examples

Example 1. Despite the heavy rain, the match ___ on time. (a) start (b) started (c) starts (d) starting
Solution: 'Despite the rain' signals a past event, so the past tense 'started'.
Answer: (b) started
Example 2. The speaker's ___ speech kept the audience hooked. (a) monotonous (b) captivating (c) vague (d) tedious
Solution: 'Kept hooked' needs a positive word: captivating.
Answer: (b) captivating
Example 3. If I ___ earlier, I would have caught the train. (a) leave (b) left (c) had left (d) have left
Solution: Third conditional: had left.
Answer: (c) had left
Example 4. Neither of the answers ___ correct. (a) are (b) is (c) were (d) be
Solution: 'Neither' is singular, so 'is'.
Answer: (b) is

Shortcuts

  • Decide the blank type in five seconds.
  • Eliminate first, choose second.
  • If unsure after 25 s, guess and move on.

Common mistakes

  • Spending 40 seconds on one item.
  • Going back to a skipped item and losing time on the next.
  • Choosing an option that is grammatical but changes the meaning.

Passage Recall

Passage Recall is a memory-plus-writing task. A short passage appears for 30 seconds and then disappears; you then have 90 seconds to type what you remember. There are 4 such tasks, each worth the same time (120 seconds).

Start from zero

How the task works

You read a short passage on screen. After 30 seconds the text disappears. You then type the passage from memory in 90 seconds. The score depends on how many key facts you recover correctly and how clearly you write them.

The 30-second reading plan

Do not try to memorise word for word. In the first pass, spot the structure: who, what, when, where, how many. Group the passage into three or four chunks. Pay special attention to names, numbers, dates and the order of events.
The structure of a recall task

The 90-second writing plan

Write the most important facts first, in the same order as the passage. Use short sentences. Keep names and numbers exact. If you forget a detail, skip it and continue; do not invent facts. Use the last ten seconds to fix spelling.

Memory tricks

Turn numbers into a small story (3 March, 6 weeks). Make a mental picture of names and places. Repeat the key words in your head while reading. Practise with different topics: news, notices, instructions, processes.

Typing practice

You must type fast and accurately. Practise on a keyboard for 10 minutes daily. Aim for 30 or more words per minute with few errors.

Formula and rule box

  • Passage visible: 30 seconds
  • Writing time: 90 seconds
  • Total per task: 120 seconds
  • Capture: who, what, when, where, how many
  • Write: short sentences, same order, exact names and numbers
  • Never add facts you did not read

Step-by-step method

  1. Read once, find the structure and underline mentally.
  2. Chunk the passage into 3–4 groups.
  3. Write the main facts in order within 90 seconds.
  4. Fix spelling in the last seconds.

Solved examples

Example 1. Practise: 'Meera runs a bakery in Jaipur. Every morning she bakes 120 loaves and sells them by noon. Last week she added two new cakes, which sold out in three days.' (read 30 s, then recall)
Solution: Key facts: Meera, bakery, Jaipur, 120 loaves, sells by noon, two new cakes, sold out in three days. Model recall: 'Meera has a bakery in Jaipur. She bakes 120 loaves every morning and sells them by noon. Last week she added two new cakes, which sold out in three days.'
Answer: Model recall as above
Example 2. Practise: 'The company will hold a hiring drive on 22 March at its Hyderabad office. Candidates with 0 to 2 years of experience can apply. Registration closes on 15 March.'
Solution: Key facts: company, hiring drive, 22 March, Hyderabad, 0–2 years, registration closes 15 March. Model recall: 'The company is holding a hiring drive on 22 March at its Hyderabad office. People with 0 to 2 years of experience can apply. Registration closes on 15 March.'
Answer: Model recall as above
Example 3. Practise: 'Mount Kala, 5,200 metres high, is climbed by about 800 trekkers every year. The best season is September to November. Trekkers must carry a permit and a first-aid kit.'
Solution: Key facts: Mount Kala, 5,200 m, 800 trekkers, September–November, permit, first-aid kit. Model recall: 'Mount Kala is 5,200 metres high and about 800 trekkers climb it every year. The best season is September to November. Trekkers must carry a permit and a first-aid kit.'
Answer: Model recall as above

Shortcuts

  • Capture numbers and names first; fill the rest from logic.
  • Write in the same order as the passage.
  • If you forget a part, move on; partial recall still scores.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to memorise word for word.
  • Writing long sentences and running out of time.
  • Adding details that were not in the passage.

Email Writing

The Verbal section ends with one email-writing task of 9 minutes. A clear structure, a polite tone and correct grammar score well. A fixed template makes it easy.

Start from zero

The six-part structure

1. Subject line: short and specific. 2. Greeting: Dear Mr/Ms Surname (or Dear Sir/Madam). 3. Purpose: say why you are writing in the first sentence. 4. Details: dates, facts, reasons. 5. Action or request: what you want the reader to do. 6. Closing: thanks and sign-off (Regards or Yours sincerely, then your name).

The 9-minute plan

Spend 1 minute reading the task and noting the points to include. Spend 6 minutes writing. Spend 2 minutes proofreading for spelling, punctuation and missing points. Keep the email around 120 to 150 words.
Anatomy of a good email

Tone

Use a polite, formal tone. Avoid slang and short forms (use 'I am' not 'I'm' if you can). Use phrases like 'I would be grateful if', 'Kindly', 'Please let me know'. Be positive even in a complaint.

Common email types

Leave request: dates, reason, handover. Complaint: problem, evidence, expected solution. Follow-up: refer to the earlier message, ask for status. Apology: admit, explain briefly, give a fix. Information or announcement: what, when, where, who should act. Request: what you need, by when, why.

Useful phrases

Opening: I am writing to request / inform / follow up on ... Details: Due to ... / As per ... Request: Kindly arrange ... / I would appreciate it if ... Closing: Thank you for your time and consideration. I look forward to your reply.

Formula and rule box

  • Subject → Greeting → Purpose → Details → Action → Closing
  • 120–150 words in 9 minutes
  • 1 min plan + 6 min write + 2 min proofread
  • Formal tone: no slang, no abbreviations
  • State the purpose in the first sentence
  • One paragraph per idea

Step-by-step method

  1. List the points the prompt wants (dates, reason, request).
  2. Write the subject line first.
  3. Write the body in 3 short paragraphs.
  4. Proofread for spelling and tone.

Solved examples

Example 1. Write an email to your manager requesting two days' leave on 14 and 15 March for a family function.
Solution: Subject: Request for two days' leave. Dear Mr Sharma, I am writing to request leave on 14 and 15 March because of a family function. I will complete my pending tasks before I leave and share handover notes with Anita. Please let me know if you need anything else. Thank you for your consideration. Regards, Rahul
Answer: Model email
Example 2. Write an email complaining about a delayed delivery of order No. 4521.
Solution: Subject: Delay in delivery of order 4521. Dear Customer Support, I placed order 4521 on 5 June, and it was due on 9 June. It has not yet arrived. Kindly check the status and deliver it by 12 June, or arrange a refund. Thank you for your help. Yours sincerely, Anita
Answer: Model email
Example 3. Write a follow-up email after an interview on 10 July.
Solution: Subject: Follow-up on interview of 10 July. Dear Ms Rao, Thank you for interviewing me on 10 July for the Analyst role. I enjoyed learning about the team. I would appreciate an update on the next steps. Thank you for your time. Regards, Karan
Answer: Model email
Example 4. Write an apology for missing a meeting.
Solution: Subject: Apology for missing today's meeting. Dear Team, I apologise for missing today's 3 pm meeting due to an unexpected power failure. I have read the minutes and will complete my action items by tomorrow. Please let me know if I should join a call. Regards, Neha
Answer: Model email

Shortcuts

  • Memorise a single template and plug in the details.
  • Mention every point in the prompt; each is a scoring item.
  • Keep sentences under 20 words.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the subject line.
  • Writing in an informal tone.
  • Running out of time before the closing.

Cheat sheet

Every key rule and formula of the section on a few pages. Revise it daily in the last two weeks.

1. Tenses

  • since + point in time; for + duration
  • by the time + past perfect (earlier) and past (later)
  • If + present → will; If + past → would; If + past perfect → would have
  • I wish + past (present wish); I wish + past perfect (past wish)
  • Reported speech: present → past, past → past perfect
  • Universal truths stay in the simple present

2. Subject–Verb Agreement

  • each / every / either / neither → singular
  • The number of → singular; A number of → plural
  • X along with Y → verb agrees with X
  • Either X or Y / Neither X nor Y → verb agrees with Y
  • Uncountable nouns (information, advice, news) → singular
  • Subjects of one unit (bread and butter) → singular

3. Articles

  • a / an → first mention, singular, one of many
  • the → specific, unique, superlative, rivers, ranges
  • Zero article → plural/general, sports, meals, languages
  • an before vowel sounds: an hour, an MBA
  • a before consonant sounds: a university, a European

4. Prepositions

  • at + clock time; on + day/date; in + month/year
  • for + duration; since + starting point
  • between (2) / among (3+)
  • prefer X to Y (not 'than')
  • senior/junior/superior/inferior + to
  • different from; similar to

5. Pronouns and Conjunctions

  • Subject form before verb; object form after verb/preposition
  • than / as + subject form if the verb is implied
  • who (subject), whom (object), whose (possession)
  • its = belonging to it; it's = it is
  • either … or; neither … nor; both … and; not only … but also
  • although/though ≠ but
  • unless = if … not

6. Vocabulary and Word Choice

  • Find the clue word in the sentence
  • Positive clue → positive word; negative clue → negative word
  • Use the root and prefix: un-, in-, dis- often mean not
  • Eliminate options with the wrong tone (positive/negative)
  • affect = verb, effect = noun (most of the time)

7. Idioms and Phrasal Verbs

  • put off = postpone
  • call off = cancel
  • turn down = reject
  • turn up = arrive
  • look after = take care of
  • carry out = perform
  • give up = quit
  • break down = stop working

8. Error Spotting and Sentence Structure

  • Scan order: SV, tense, article, preposition, pronoun, comparison, order, parallelism
  • No double comparative: smarter, not more smarter
  • Uncountable nouns: furniture, information, advice, news → singular, no plural form
  • prefer … to; senior/junior to
  • Lists must be parallel in form
