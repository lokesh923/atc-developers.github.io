Number System

Every other topic in Numerical Ability sits on top of numbers. Here we learn what kinds of numbers exist, how to test divisibility quickly, how to find remainders and unit digits, and how to count factors. These ideas appear directly in questions and silently inside others (LCM, ratios, P and C).

Start from zero

Types of numbers

Natural numbers are 1, 2, 3 … Whole numbers add 0. Integers add negatives (… −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 …). Rational numbers can be written as p/q (like 3/4, 0.25, 5). Irrational numbers cannot (√2, π). A prime has exactly two factors (1 and itself): 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 … 2 is the only even prime. A composite has more than two factors. 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Remainders

When a is divided by n, the leftover is the remainder. A key idea: the remainder of a product equals the remainder of the product of remainders. Example: 17 × 23 divided by 5. 17 leaves 2, 23 leaves 3, so 2 × 3 = 6, which leaves 1. For powers, find the repeating pattern: 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8≡1 (mod 7), so powers of 2 repeat every 3 steps when divided by 7.

Unit digit and cyclicity

Powers of a number repeat their last digit in a cycle. Digits 0, 1, 5, 6 never change. Digits 4 and 9 cycle in 2 steps. Digits 2, 3, 7, 8 cycle in 4 steps. Divide the power by the cycle length; the remainder tells the position (remainder 0 means the last item of the cycle).

Factors

Write N as a product of prime powers: N = pᵃ × qᵇ × rᶜ. Then the number of factors is (a+1)(b+1)(c+1). The sum of factors is (p⁰+…+pᵃ)(q⁰+…+qᵇ)(r⁰+…+rᶜ). Numbers like 36, 49, 100 (perfect squares) have an odd number of factors.

Trailing zeros and bases

Each trailing zero needs a pair 2 × 5, and 5s are rarer, so the zeros in n! equal ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + …. To convert decimal to binary, divide repeatedly by 2 and read the remainders from bottom to top.
Unit-digit cycle of 7 (cyclicity)

Formula box

  • Number of factors of N = pᵃqᵇrᶜ → (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)
  • Sum of factors = [(pᵃ⁺¹−1)/(p−1)] × [(qᵇ⁺¹−1)/(q−1)] × …
  • Trailing zeros of n! = ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + …
  • Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder
  • Sum of first n natural numbers = n(n+1)/2
  • Sum of first n odd numbers = n²; first n even numbers = n(n+1)
  • (a+b)² = a²+2ab+b², (a−b)² = a²−2ab+b², a²−b² = (a+b)(a−b)

Step-by-step method

  1. Check divisibility with the rules before dividing.
  2. For remainders of products or sums, reduce each number first, then combine.
  3. For unit digits of aⁿ, take the unit digit of a, find its cycle, reduce n modulo the cycle length.
  4. For factor counts, always write the prime factorisation first.

Solved examples

Example 1. Find the unit digit of 7¹²³.
Solution: The unit digit of powers of 7 cycles 7, 9, 3, 1 (length 4). Divide 123 by 4: remainder 3. The 3rd item of the cycle is 3.
Answer: 3
Example 2. How many factors does 360 have? What is their sum?
Solution: 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹. Count = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4×3×2 = 24. Sum = (2⁴−1)/(2−1) × (3³−1)/(3−1) × (5²−1)/(5−1) = 15 × 13 × 6 = 1170.
Answer: 24 factors, sum 1170
Example 3. How many trailing zeros are in 100!?
Solution: ⌊100/5⌋ = 20, ⌊100/25⌋ = 4, ⌊100/125⌋ = 0. Total = 20 + 4 = 24.
Answer: 24

Shortcuts

  • Memorise primes up to 100 (25 of them).
  • For remainder by 9, use the digit sum. For remainder by 11, use the alternating sum.
  • A number is divisible by 12 if it is divisible by both 3 and 4 (not by 2 and 6).

Common mistakes

  • Treating 1 as a prime number.
  • Using cycle length 4 for digit 4 or 9 (their cycle is 2).
  • Forgetting that remainder 0 in cyclicity means the last item of the cycle.

LCM and HCF

HCF (highest common factor) is the largest number that divides all the given numbers. LCM (lowest common multiple) is the smallest number that all of them divide. Real situations: cutting ribbons into equal pieces (HCF) and bells ringing together (LCM).

Start from zero

Prime factorisation method

Write each number as a product of primes. HCF takes the lowest power of each common prime. LCM takes the highest power of every prime that appears. Example: 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3². HCF = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6. LCM = 2² × 3² = 36.

Product rule

For two numbers: a × b = HCF × LCM. So if you know three of these four values you can find the fourth. This rule is for two numbers only.
Prime factors of 12 and 18: HCF is the overlap, LCM is everything

HCF and LCM of fractions

HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators. LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators (fractions in lowest terms).

Word problem patterns

Greatest number dividing a, b, c leaving the same remainder r → HCF of (a−r, b−r, c−r). Leaving remainders r₁, r₂, r₃ → HCF of (a−r₁, b−r₂, c−r₃). Smallest number leaving remainder r with divisors x, y, z → LCM(x, y, z) + r. Events repeating together (bells, lights, runners on a track) → LCM of their time gaps. Equal division (tiles, ribbons, rows) → HCF.

Formula box

  • HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers
  • HCF ≤ every given number ≤ LCM
  • HCF of fractions = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators)
  • LCM of fractions = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators)
  • Smallest number leaving remainder r = LCM + r
  • Greatest number leaving remainder r = HCF(a−r, b−r, …)

Step-by-step method

  1. Decide whether the story asks for the biggest equal piece (HCF) or the first common time (LCM).
  2. Factorise each number into primes.
  3. Apply lowest powers (HCF) or highest powers (LCM).
  4. For remainder problems, adjust the numbers first, then use HCF or LCM.

Solved examples

Example 1. Find the HCF and LCM of 12 and 18.
Solution: 12 = 2² × 3, 18 = 2 × 3². HCF = 2 × 3 = 6. LCM = 2² × 3² = 36. Check: 6 × 36 = 216 = 12 × 18.
Answer: HCF 6, LCM 36
Example 2. Find the greatest number that divides 60 and 76 leaving remainder 4 each time.
Solution: Subtract the remainder: 60 − 4 = 56 and 76 − 4 = 72. HCF(56, 72) = 8.
Answer: 8
Example 3. Three bells ring every 12, 15 and 20 minutes. They ring together at 9:00 AM. When will they next ring together?
Solution: LCM(12, 15, 20): 12 = 2²×3, 15 = 3×5, 20 = 2²×5. LCM = 2²×3×5 = 60. They ring together again after 60 minutes, at 10:00 AM.
Answer: 10:00 AM
Example 4. Find the smallest number which leaves remainder 5 when divided by 6, 8 and 12.
Solution: LCM(6, 8, 12) = 24. Required number = 24 + 5 = 29.
Answer: 29

Shortcuts

  • For two co-prime numbers, HCF = 1 and LCM = their product.
  • If the ratio of two numbers is a:b with HCF h, the numbers are ha and hb, and LCM = hab.
  • Greatest 4-digit number divisible by x: 9999 − (9999 mod x). Smallest: 1000 + (x − 1000 mod x) when 1000 is not divisible.

Common mistakes

  • Using HCF when the question wants LCM (check the story).
  • Adding the remainder in the HCF pattern instead of subtracting it.
  • Applying HCF × LCM = product to three numbers (it works for two only).

Percentages

Percent means 'per hundred'. Percentages are the language of profit, discount, interest, data tables and population questions, so speed here pays off across the whole paper.

Start from zero

The basic idea

x% means x/100. To find x% of a number N, compute (x/100) × N. To find what percent a is of b, compute (a/b) × 100. Example: 35% of 240 = 0.35 × 240 = 84. And 72 is (72/90) × 100 = 80% of 90.

Increase and decrease

New value = Old × (1 ± r/100). An increase of 20% means multiply by 1.2. A decrease of 20% means multiply by 0.8. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the start: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, a 4% net fall.

Successive percentage change

For two changes of a% and b%, net change = a + b + (ab/100). Use a plus sign for an increase and a minus sign for a decrease. Example: +20% then −10%: 20 − 10 − 2 = +8%.

The reverse percentage

If A is x% more than B, then B is [x/(100+x)] × 100 % less than A. If A is x% less than B, then B is [x/(100−x)] × 100 % more than A. Example: A earns 25% more than B, so B earns 25/125 = 20% less than A.

Price and consumption

If the price rises by r%, a household must cut consumption by [r/(100+r)] × 100 % to keep spending the same. A 25% rise needs a 20% cut. A 20% rise needs a 16.67% cut.
Successive change: +20% then −10% gives +8%, not +10%

Formula box

  • x% of N = (x/100) × N
  • Percentage change = (change / original) × 100
  • Successive change = a + b + ab/100
  • If A is x% more than B: B is x/(100+x) × 100 % less than A
  • Population after n years = P (1 + r/100)ⁿ
  • Depreciated value = P (1 − r/100)ⁿ
  • Reduce consumption by r/(100+r) × 100 % when price rises r%

Step-by-step method

  1. Convert the percentage to a fraction where possible (25% = 1/4).
  2. Identify the base (the 'of' number) before computing.
  3. For two or more changes, use the successive-change formula.
  4. Check whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than the start.

Solved examples

Example 1. The price of an item rises by 25%. By what percent must a family cut consumption to keep the expenditure the same?
Solution: Cut = 25/(100+25) × 100 = 25/125 × 100 = 20%.
Answer: 20%
Example 2. A number is increased by 20% and then decreased by 10%. Find the net change.
Solution: Net = 20 − 10 + (20 × −10)/100 = 10 − 2 = +8%.
Answer: 8% increase
Example 3. The population of a town is 40,000 and grows 10% per year. Find the population after 2 years.
Solution: 40,000 × (1.1)² = 40,000 × 1.21 = 48,400.
Answer: 48,400
Example 4. A candidate gets 45% of votes in a two-candidate election and loses by 300 votes. Find the total votes.
Solution: The winner gets 55%. Difference = 55 − 45 = 10% of total = 300. Total = 3,000.
Answer: 3,000

Shortcuts

  • x% of y = y% of x. So 8% of 50 = 50% of 8 = 4.
  • To find 15% of a number: 10% + 5% (half of 10%).
  • For 'k% more', multiply by (100+k)/100. For 'k% less', multiply by (100−k)/100.

Common mistakes

  • Taking the percentage on the wrong base (old vs new value).
  • Adding successive percentages directly (20% then −20% is not 0).
  • Confusing percentage points with percent (from 40% to 50% is +10 points but +25%).

Profit and Loss

Cost price, selling price, marked price, discount: the vocabulary of every shop. This topic is a favourite because it combines percentages with simple logic.

Start from zero

The four key words

Cost Price (CP) is what the seller pays. Selling Price (SP) is what the seller receives. Marked Price (MP) is the price printed on the tag. Discount = MP − SP. If SP > CP there is a profit; if SP < CP there is a loss.

Profit and loss percentages

Profit = SP − CP, and Profit % = (Profit / CP) × 100. Loss % = (Loss / CP) × 100. Always calculate profit or loss percentage on the cost price, unless the question says otherwise. Discount percentage is always on the marked price.
Marked price, discount, selling price, cost and profit on one scale

Finding SP or CP quickly

SP = CP × (100 + P)/100 for a profit of P%. SP = CP × (100 − L)/100 for a loss of L%. So CP = SP × 100/(100 + P). Example: SP = ₹540 at a 10% loss gives CP = 540 × 100/90 = ₹600.

Discounts

SP = MP × (100 − d)/100. For successive discounts of a% and b%, the single equivalent discount = a + b − ab/100. Example: 20% and 10% make 20 + 10 − 2 = 28%.

False weights

A dishonest dealer sells at cost price but gives less goods. Gain % = [error / (true weight − error)] × 100. Selling 800 g as 1 kg: error 200, gain = 200/800 × 100 = 25%.

Same selling price, equal gain and loss

If two articles are sold at the same price, one at x% gain and the other at x% loss, there is always an overall loss of x²/100 %. At 20%: loss = 400/100 = 4%.

Formula box

  • Profit % = (SP − CP)/CP × 100
  • SP = CP × (100 ± P)/100
  • CP = SP × 100/(100 ± P)
  • Discount % = (MP − SP)/MP × 100
  • Successive discount = a + b − ab/100
  • Loss % when equal gain and loss % = x²/100
  • Gain % on false weight = error/(true − error) × 100

Step-by-step method

  1. List CP, SP, MP, discount clearly.
  2. Pick the base for each percentage (CP for profit, MP for discount).
  3. Use multiplication factors (1.2, 0.9) to chain steps.
  4. If CP is unknown, assume CP = 100 and scale up.

Solved examples

Example 1. An article costing ₹500 is sold for ₹575. Find the profit percentage.
Solution: Profit = 575 − 500 = 75. Profit % = 75/500 × 100 = 15%.
Answer: 15%
Example 2. The marked price is ₹800 and a 15% discount is given. If CP is ₹600, find the profit %.
Solution: SP = 800 × 0.85 = ₹680. Profit = 680 − 600 = 80. Profit % = 80/600 × 100 = 13.33%.
Answer: 13.33%
Example 3. Two successive discounts of 20% and 10% are offered on ₹2,500. Find the final price.
Solution: Net discount = 20 + 10 − 2 = 28%. Price = 2,500 × 0.72 = ₹1,800.
Answer: ₹1,800
Example 4. A shopkeeper marks goods 25% above cost and then gives a 20% discount. Find the profit or loss %.
Solution: Take CP = 100. MP = 125. SP = 125 × 0.8 = 100. Profit = 0.
Answer: No profit, no loss

Shortcuts

  • If CP of x articles = SP of y articles, profit % = (x − y)/y × 100.
  • Marking up m% and giving d% discount: net % = m − d − md/100.
  • Use CP = 100 as an assumed value when only percentages are given.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating profit % on the selling price.
  • Applying discount on the cost price instead of the marked price.
  • Adding successive discounts directly (20% + 10% is not 30%).

Time, Speed and Distance

Speed questions test how well you handle ratios and units. Trains, boats and races are the three standard settings.

Start from zero

The core relation

Speed = Distance / Time. So Distance = Speed × Time and Time = Distance / Speed. Keep units consistent. To convert km/h to m/s multiply by 5/18. To convert m/s to km/h multiply by 18/5. Example: 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s.

Average speed

Average speed = Total distance / Total time. It is not the average of the two speeds. If equal distances are covered at speeds x and y, the average speed = 2xy/(x + y). Example: going at 40 and returning at 60 gives 2×40×60/100 = 48 km/h.
A train must travel its own length plus the platform

Relative speed

When two objects move in the same direction the relative speed is the difference of speeds. When they move in opposite directions, add the speeds. Relative speed turns a two-body problem into a one-body problem.

Trains

A train crossing a pole or a person travels its own length. A train crossing a platform or bridge travels its length plus the platform length. Two trains crossing each other travel the sum of their lengths at the relative speed.

Boats and streams

Downstream speed = b + s. Upstream speed = b − s. Here b is boat speed in still water and s is the stream speed. So b = (down + up)/2 and s = (down − up)/2.

Speed, time and distance ratios

For a fixed distance, speed and time are inversely proportional. If speed is 4/5 of usual, time is 5/4 of usual. For a fixed time, distance is proportional to speed.
Distance-time graph and boat/stream directions

Formula box

  • Speed = Distance/Time
  • km/h → m/s: × 5/18; m/s → km/h: × 18/5
  • Average speed (equal distances) = 2xy/(x+y)
  • Same direction: relative speed = |u − v|; opposite: u + v
  • Train crossing a platform: (L + P)/speed
  • Two trains: (L₁ + L₂)/relative speed
  • Boat: b = (d+u)/2, s = (d−u)/2

Step-by-step method

  1. Convert all units to a common system before calculating.
  2. For trains, decide what distance is covered (own length, or length plus platform).
  3. For meeting problems, use the sum of speeds; for chase problems, the difference.
  4. Use ratio shortcuts: speed ratio a:b means time ratio b:a for the same distance.

Solved examples

Example 1. A train 150 m long at 54 km/h crosses a platform 250 m long. Find the time taken.
Solution: Speed = 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s. Distance = 150 + 250 = 400 m. Time = 400/15 = 26.67 s.
Answer: 26.67 s
Example 2. A boat goes 45 km downstream and 45 km upstream. Boat speed in still water is 12 km/h and stream speed is 3 km/h. Find the total time.
Solution: Down = 15 km/h, time = 3 h. Up = 9 km/h, time = 5 h. Total = 8 h.
Answer: 8 hours
Example 3. Two trains of lengths 100 m and 120 m run in opposite directions at 40 km/h and 50 km/h. Find the time to cross each other.
Solution: Relative speed = 90 km/h = 25 m/s. Distance = 220 m. Time = 220/25 = 8.8 s.
Answer: 8.8 s
Example 4. A person walks at 4/5 of his usual speed and reaches 10 minutes late. Find his usual time.
Solution: New time = 5/4 of usual. Extra time = 1/4 of usual = 10 min, so usual time = 40 min.
Answer: 40 minutes

Shortcuts

  • 72 km/h = 20 m/s and 54 km/h = 15 m/s. Remember 36 km/h = 10 m/s.
  • If a train passes two objects of lengths, subtract the times to get the length difference.
  • In a race of 100 m, 'A beats B by 10 m' means B has run 90 m when A finishes.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging speeds for the whole journey.
  • Forgetting to add the platform length for the train.
  • Mixing minutes and hours or km and m.

Time and Work

Work problems are rate problems in disguise. Each worker has a rate (work per day), and rates simply add up when people work together.

Start from zero

Work rate

If A finishes a job in a days, A's one-day work is 1/a. Together A and B in one day do 1/a + 1/b, so together they need ab/(a + b) days. Example: A in 10 days and B in 15 days: 150/25 = 6 days.

The efficiency (LCM) method

Take the LCM of the days as the total work in 'units'. Then each person's efficiency is units per day. Example: A in 12 days, B in 18, C in 36: total = 36 units. A = 3, B = 2, C = 1 units/day. Together 6 units/day, so 36/6 = 6 days.
Pipes add (inlet) or subtract (outlet) their rates

Men, days and hours

Work is proportional to men × days × hours. M₁D₁H₁ / W₁ = M₂D₂H₂ / W₂. More men means fewer days (inverse), more work means more days (direct).

Pipes and cisterns

An inlet pipe adds water (positive rate). An outlet or leak removes water (negative rate). Net rate = sum of rates. Example: A fills in 6 h, B in 8 h, C empties in 12 h. Net = 1/6 + 1/8 − 1/12 = 5/24 per hour, so 4.8 h.

Alternate days and leaving early

If A and B work on alternate days, compute one 2-day cycle, count full cycles, then handle the remainder. If someone leaves early, split the total time into stages and add the work done in each stage.

Wages

Money is divided in proportion to the work done, which is rate × days.
The efficiency method: one unit of work per person per day

Formula box

  • A and B together = ab/(a+b) days
  • If A alone takes a and A+B take t days, B alone takes at/(a − t)
  • Work = Men × Days × Hours (proportional)
  • Inlet pipe = + rate, outlet pipe = − rate
  • Wages ratio = ratio of (rate × days worked)
  • Total work = LCM of days (efficiency method)

Step-by-step method

  1. Choose total work = LCM of the days.
  2. Convert every worker's time into units per day.
  3. Add or subtract rates (outlets subtract).
  4. Divide the total units by the combined rate.

Solved examples

Example 1. A can finish a job in 10 days and B in 15 days. Find the time taken working together.
Solution: Rates: 1/10 + 1/15 = 5/30 = 1/6 per day. Time = 6 days.
Answer: 6 days
Example 2. A and B together finish a job in 12 days. A alone takes 20 days. How long will B alone take?
Solution: B's rate = 1/12 − 1/20 = (5 − 3)/60 = 1/30. B alone takes 30 days.
Answer: 30 days
Example 3. 8 men build a wall in 12 days. How many days will 6 men need?
Solution: 8 × 12 = 6 × D, so D = 16 days.
Answer: 16 days
Example 4. Pipe A fills a tank in 6 h, pipe B in 8 h and pipe C empties it in 12 h. All three are opened. Find the time to fill it.
Solution: Net rate = 1/6 + 1/8 − 1/12 = (4 + 3 − 2)/24 = 5/24 per hour. Time = 24/5 = 4.8 h.
Answer: 4.8 hours

Shortcuts

  • Work done in a part: time/total time gives the fraction (3/5 of work takes 3/5 of the time).
  • For 'A and B alternate', one cycle = rate of A + rate of B.
  • If efficiency ratio is a:b, time ratio is b:a.

Common mistakes

  • Adding days instead of adding daily work rates.
  • Forgetting to subtract an outlet pipe.
  • Splitting wages in the ratio of days instead of work done.

Ratio and Proportion

A ratio compares two quantities. A proportion says two ratios are equal. These ideas support partnership, mixtures, ages and almost every word problem.

Start from zero

Ratio

The ratio of a to b is written a : b = a/b. Both quantities must be in the same unit. Simplify by dividing both sides by their HCF. Example: 24 : 36 = 2 : 3.

Combining ratios

If A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5, make B equal in both: A : B = 8 : 12, B : C = 12 : 15. So A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15. The compound ratio of a:b and c:d is ac : bd.
₹1,200 split 3 : 5 : 2 as a bar and as a pie

Dividing a quantity

To split an amount N in the ratio a : b : c, find total parts (a + b + c). Each part = N/(a + b + c). Then multiply. Example: ₹1,200 in 3 : 5 : 2 gives ₹360, ₹600 and ₹240.

Proportion

If a : b = c : d then ad = bc. Here d is the fourth proportional. The mean proportional between a and b is √(ab). The third proportional to a and b is b²/a.

Direct and inverse proportion

In direct variation, if one quantity doubles, the other doubles (cost vs quantity). In inverse variation, if one doubles, the other halves (speed vs time, men vs days).

Useful algebra

If a/b = c/d then (a + b)/(a − b) = (c + d)/(c − d) (componendo and dividendo). If ratio is 3 : 4, assume actual values 3x and 4x and solve with the given total or difference.

Formula box

  • a : b = a/b
  • Compound ratio of a:b and c:d = ac : bd
  • If a:b = c:d then ad = bc
  • Mean proportional = √(ab)
  • Third proportional to a, b = b²/a
  • Amount to each = N × (part / total parts)
  • Direct: a₁/b₁ = a₂/b₂; Inverse: a₁b₁ = a₂b₂

Step-by-step method

  1. Express unknowns as multiples (3x, 4x).
  2. Use the given sum or difference to find x.
  3. Make common terms equal when combining ratios.
  4. For new ratios after adding a value, form an equation.

Solved examples

Example 1. Divide ₹1,200 among A, B and C in the ratio 3 : 5 : 2.
Solution: Total parts = 10. One part = 120. A = 360, B = 600, C = 240.
Answer: ₹360, ₹600, ₹240
Example 2. If A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5, find A : B : C.
Solution: Make B the same: A : B = 8 : 12 and B : C = 12 : 15. So A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15.
Answer: 8 : 12 : 15
Example 3. The ratio of boys to girls is 7 : 5 and the total is 360. If 30 more girls join, find the new ratio.
Solution: Boys = 210, girls = 150. After 30 girls join: 210 : 180 = 7 : 6.
Answer: 7 : 6
Example 4. Two numbers are in the ratio 5 : 7. If 6 is added to each, the ratio becomes 8 : 11. Find the numbers.
Solution: (5x + 6)/(7x + 6) = 8/11. 55x + 66 = 56x + 48, so x = 18. Numbers are 90 and 126.
Answer: 90 and 126

Shortcuts

  • If x : y = a : b, the value of x/(x+y) is a/(a+b).
  • For three-term ratios with a common middle term, multiply cross.
  • If 2A = 3B = 4C, then A : B : C = 1/2 : 1/3 : 1/4 = 6 : 4 : 3.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing quantities in different units.
  • Not making the common term equal before combining ratios.
  • Treating 'ratio 3 : 4' as actual values 3 and 4.

Averages

An average describes a whole group with a single number. Average questions are fast marks if you remember to work with totals rather than averages.

Start from zero

The definition

Average = Sum of all values / Number of values. So Sum = Average × Number. Always convert averages into sums; sums are easy to add and subtract.

Averages of series

The average of the first n natural numbers is (n+1)/2. For the first n odd numbers it is n. For the first n even numbers it is (n+1). For consecutive numbers, the average is the middle value.
The average (dashed line) balances the values above and below it

Weighted average

When groups have different sizes, weight each average by its size. Weighted average = (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂ + …)/(n₁ + n₂ + …). Example: 20 students at average 60 and 30 at average 70 gives (1,200 + 2,100)/50 = 66.

Adding, removing, replacing

Adding a new value: new sum = old sum + new value. Removing: subtract. Replacing a value by another changes the sum by (new − old); the average changes by (new − old)/n.

Average speed and ages

Average speed uses total distance/total time (see Time, Speed and Distance). For ages, when everyone gets older by t years, the average also rises by t years. When a person joins or leaves, recompute with the total age.

Formula box

  • Average = Sum/Number of items
  • Sum = Average × Number
  • Average of first n natural numbers = (n+1)/2
  • Weighted average = Σ(nᵢaᵢ)/Σnᵢ
  • Change in average on replacing = (new − old)/n
  • New value joining = old average + n × (change in average)

Step-by-step method

  1. Convert each average into a sum.
  2. Add or subtract sums according to the story.
  3. Divide by the new count to get the new average.
  4. Use change-in-average shortcuts for replacements.

Solved examples

Example 1. The average of 5 numbers is 20. A sixth number is added and the average becomes 22. Find the sixth number.
Solution: Old sum = 100. New sum = 22 × 6 = 132. Sixth number = 132 − 100 = 32.
Answer: 32
Example 2. Class A has 20 students with average 60. Class B has 30 students with average 70. Find the combined average.
Solution: Sum = 20×60 + 30×70 = 1,200 + 2,100 = 3,300. Average = 3,300/50 = 66.
Answer: 66
Example 3. The average age of 10 persons is 30. A 45-year-old person is replaced by a 25-year-old. Find the new average.
Solution: Change in sum = 25 − 45 = −20. New average = 30 − 20/10 = 28.
Answer: 28
Example 4. A batsman's average after 10 innings is 40. He scores 62 in the 11th innings. Find his new average.
Solution: Total = 400 + 62 = 462. New average = 462/11 = 42.
Answer: 42

Shortcuts

  • The weighted average always lies between the two group averages, closer to the larger group.
  • Average of an AP = (first + last)/2.
  • If every value increases by k, the average increases by k.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging the averages without weights.
  • Forgetting to update the count when a value is added or removed.
  • Dividing by the old number of items.

Simple and Compound Interest

Interest is the price of using money. Simple interest grows in a straight line; compound interest grows faster because interest earns interest.

Start from zero

Simple Interest

SI = P × R × T / 100, where P is the principal, R is the yearly rate in percent and T is the time in years. Amount A = P + SI. Example: ₹5,000 at 8% for 3 years gives SI = 5,000 × 8 × 3/100 = ₹1,200.

Compound Interest

Interest is added to the principal every period. A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ and CI = A − P. Example: ₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years: A = 10,000 × 1.21 = ₹12,100, so CI = ₹2,100 (SI would be ₹2,000).
SI grows as a line; CI curves upward because interest earns interest

Different compounding periods

For half-yearly compounding use rate R/2 and 2n periods. For quarterly use R/4 and 4n. Example: ₹8,000 at 10% for 1 year compounded half-yearly: 5% for 2 periods = 8,000 × 1.1025 = ₹8,820.

CI and SI difference

For 2 years the difference CI − SI = P(R/100)². For 3 years it is P(R/100)² × (300 + R)/100. Example: ₹8,000 at 5% for 2 years: 8,000 × (0.05)² = ₹20.

Doubling and tripling

With SI, a sum becomes n times itself in 100(n−1)/R years. At 20% SI a sum doubles in 5 years. Under compound interest, use the rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72/R.

Formula box

  • SI = PRT/100
  • A = P + SI
  • A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ (compound)
  • CI = A − P
  • Half-yearly: rate R/2, periods 2n
  • Quarterly: rate R/4, periods 4n
  • CI − SI (2 yrs) = P(R/100)²
  • CI − SI (3 yrs) = P(R/100)²(300 + R)/100

Step-by-step method

  1. Check whether interest is simple or compound.
  2. Match the rate period with the compounding period.
  3. Use the multiplier (1 + R/100) for each period.
  4. For differences, use the shortcut formulas.

Solved examples

Example 1. Find the SI on ₹5,000 at 8% per year for 3 years.
Solution: SI = 5,000 × 8 × 3/100 = ₹1,200. Amount = ₹6,200.
Answer: SI ₹1,200
Example 2. Find the compound interest on ₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years.
Solution: A = 10,000 × (1.1)² = 12,100. CI = 12,100 − 10,000 = ₹2,100.
Answer: ₹2,100
Example 3. A sum doubles in 5 years at simple interest. Find the rate.
Solution: SI = P in 5 years: R = 100/5 = 20% per year.
Answer: 20%
Example 4. ₹8,000 is invested at 10% per year, compounded half-yearly, for 1 year. Find the amount.
Solution: Rate per period 5%, periods 2. A = 8,000 × (1.05)² = 8,000 × 1.1025 = ₹8,820.
Answer: ₹8,820

Shortcuts

  • The multipliers 10% → 1.1, 20% → 1.2, 5% → 1.05 make compounding quick.
  • For 2 years at r% CI, the net growth is 2r + r²/100 percent.
  • For CI differences use the shortcut, not the full calculation.

Common mistakes

  • Using R directly when compounding half-yearly (use R/2 and 2n).
  • Calculating CI as the final amount without subtracting the principal.
  • Mixing months and years in T.

Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is. At TCS NQT level you need the sample space of coins, dice and cards, and two simple rules: add for 'or' (mutually exclusive) and multiply for 'and' (independent).

Start from zero

Basic definition

P(E) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes. Probability lies between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). The probability that an event does not happen is 1 − P(E).

Coins

A coin has 2 outcomes. n coins have 2ⁿ outcomes. For 3 coins the outcomes are HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. P(exactly two heads) = 3/8.
Two dice: ways to get each sum, and the 6 × 6 outcome grid

Dice

One die has 6 outcomes. Two dice have 36. The number of ways to get each sum is shown in the chart below. Sum 7 is the most likely (6 ways, probability 1/6).

Cards

A deck has 52 cards in 4 suits (hearts, diamonds red; spades, clubs black), each with 13 cards (A, 2 … 10, J, Q, K). There are 12 face cards, 4 aces, 4 kings and 26 red cards.

Rules

OR rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). If A and B cannot happen together, the last term is zero. AND rule: for independent events P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). For drawing without replacement, the second probability changes: P(both red) = (5/8) × (4/7) for 5 red out of 8.

At least one

Use the complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none). Example: at least one head in 3 tosses = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
A standard deck of 52 cards

Formula box

  • P(E) = favourable/total
  • P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
  • P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)
  • P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) (independent)
  • n coins → 2ⁿ outcomes; 2 dice → 36 outcomes
  • P(at least one) = 1 − P(none)
  • Cards: 52 total, 26 red, 12 face cards, 4 aces

Step-by-step method

  1. List the total outcomes (sample space).
  2. Count favourable outcomes carefully.
  3. Use the complement for 'at least one'.
  4. For multi-stage events, multiply stage probabilities, changing counts if there is no replacement.

Solved examples

Example 1. Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is 8.
Solution: Favourable: (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2) = 5 outcomes. Probability = 5/36.
Answer: 5/36
Example 2. A card is drawn from a deck. Find the probability that it is a king or a heart.
Solution: Kings 4, hearts 13, king of hearts counted twice. Favourable = 4 + 13 − 1 = 16. Probability = 16/52 = 4/13.
Answer: 4/13
Example 3. A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find P(both red).
Solution: P = (5/8) × (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14.
Answer: 5/14
Example 4. A coin is tossed 3 times. Find P(at least one head).
Solution: P(no head) = (1/2)³ = 1/8. P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
Answer: 7/8

Shortcuts

  • Memorise the dice-sum counts: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 for sums 2 to 12.
  • P(at least one) = 1 − P(none) is almost always faster.
  • With replacement means the probabilities stay the same.

Common mistakes

  • Counting (3,5) and (5,3) as one outcome for dice.
  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap in the OR rule.
  • Not reducing the fraction at the end (TITA answers need the exact form requested).

Permutation and Combination

P and C is the mathematics of counting. It answers questions like 'in how many ways can we arrange or choose?'. The one decision to master is: does order matter (permutation) or not (combination)?

Start from zero

Counting principle

If one task can be done in m ways and a second in n ways, then doing both in order can be done in m × n ways. For three tasks it is m × n × p. Example: 3 shirts and 4 trousers give 12 outfits.

Factorial

n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1, and 0! = 1. So 5! = 120. The number of ways to arrange n different objects in a row is n!.
Slot method for arrangements, and circular seating

Permutation (order matters)

nPr = n!/(n−r)!. It counts arrangements of r objects out of n. Example: 8P3 = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336.

Combination (order does not matter)

nCr = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. It counts selections. Note nCr = nC(n−r). Example: choosing a committee of 3 from 8: 8C3 = 56.

Repeated letters and circles

For n objects where p are alike and q are alike, arrangements = n!/(p! q!). 'LEVEL' has 5 letters with L twice and E twice: 5!/(2!2!) = 30. For n people around a circular table, arrangements = (n−1)!.

Useful applications

Handshakes among n people = nC2. Diagonals of an n-sided polygon = nC2 − n. Number of ways to select with a restriction: fix the condition first, then choose from the rest.

Formula box

  • n! = n(n−1)(n−2)…1
  • nPr = n!/(n−r)!
  • nCr = n!/[r!(n−r)!]
  • nCr = nC(n−r)
  • Arrangements with repetition = n!/(p!q!…)
  • Circular arrangements = (n−1)!
  • Handshakes = nC2; Diagonals = nC2 − n

Step-by-step method

  1. Decide: arranging (order matters) or selecting (order does not)?
  2. Handle restrictions first (fix a person, glue a pair).
  3. Use nCr for groups, nPr or n! for lines.
  4. For repeated letters divide by factorials of the repeats.

Solved examples

Example 1. In how many ways can 5 different books be arranged on a shelf?
Solution: 5! = 120.
Answer: 120
Example 2. A committee of 2 men and 2 women is chosen from 5 men and 4 women. Find the number of ways.
Solution: 5C2 × 4C2 = 10 × 6 = 60.
Answer: 60
Example 3. How many ways can the letters of LEVEL be arranged?
Solution: 5 letters with L twice and E twice: 5!/(2! × 2!) = 120/4 = 30.
Answer: 30
Example 4. In how many ways can 6 people sit around a circular table?
Solution: (6 − 1)! = 120.
Answer: 120

Shortcuts

  • nC2 = n(n−1)/2 and nC3 = n(n−1)(n−2)/6.
  • Always compute nCr using the smaller of r and n−r.
  • 'At least' problems: total ways minus ways with none.

Common mistakes

  • Using permutation when only selection is needed.
  • Forgetting to divide for repeated letters.
  • Using n! instead of (n−1)! for circular seating.

  • Cylinder: V = πr²h, CSA = 2πrh
  • Cone: V = ⅓πr²h, l = √(r²+h²), CSA = πrl
  • Sphere: V = (4/3)πr³, SA = 4πr²
  • Cuboid: V = lbh; diagonal = √(l²+b²+h²)
  • Cube: V = a³, TSA = 6a²

Step-by-step method

  1. Draw a quick sketch and label measurements.
  2. Pick the formula (area, surface or volume).
  3. Use π = 22/7 when radius is a multiple of 7.
  4. Check units at the end.

Solved examples

Example 1. Find the area and circumference of a circle of radius 7 cm.
Solution: Area = 22/7 × 49 = 154 cm². Circumference = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm.
Answer: 154 cm², 44 cm
Example 2. Find the volume of a cylinder with radius 7 cm and height 10 cm.
Solution: V = 22/7 × 49 × 10 = 1,540 cm³.
Answer: 1,540 cm³
Example 3. A cone has radius 3 cm and height 4 cm. Find its slant height and volume (in terms of π).
Solution: l = √(9 + 16) = 5 cm. V = ⅓ × π × 9 × 4 = 12π cm³ ≈ 37.7 cm³.
Answer: l = 5 cm, V = 12π
Example 4. A metallic sphere of radius 6 cm is melted into smaller spheres of radius 2 cm. How many are made?
Solution: Volume ratio = (6/2)³ = 27.
Answer: 27

Shortcuts

  • Doubling the radius multiplies the circle's area by 4 and the sphere's volume by 8.
  • Cost problems: Area or perimeter × rate.
  • A rectangle with fixed perimeter has maximum area when it is a square.

Common mistakes

  • Using diameter instead of radius.
  • Mixing square and cubic units.
  • Using height instead of slant height for the curved surface area of a cone.

Mixtures and Alligation

Alligation is a fast graphical method to mix two ingredients to a required average. It is just the weighted average idea turned into a ratio trick.

Start from zero

The alligation rule

If you mix a cheaper item (price c) with a dearer item (price d) to get a mixture of mean price m, then (quantity of cheaper) : (quantity of dearer) = (d − m) : (m − c). Draw the cross: put c and d on top, m in the middle, subtract diagonally.

Mixing solutions

The same rule works for percentages of any ingredient (acid in water, milk in water). Treat each concentration like a price. Example: mixing 30% and 50% solutions to get 40% needs equal quantities because 50 − 40 = 40 − 30.
The alligation cross: subtract diagonally to get the mixing ratio

Replacement of a liquid

A vessel has V litres of pure milk. If x litres are removed and replaced with water, and this is done n times, milk left = V × (1 − x/V)ⁿ. Example: 20 L, remove 4 L twice: 20 × (0.8)² = 12.8 L.

Adding or removing water

The quantity of the other ingredient stays the same while water changes. Use that fixed quantity to find the new ratio. If 50 L of a 20% solution contains 10 L alcohol, adding water until it is 10% means 100 L total, so add 50 L water.

Cost and profit in mixing

Mixing cheap and costly items to sell at a certain price gives a profit or loss. First find the mean cost price using the alligation, then apply the profit % on it.

Formula box

  • Cheaper : Dearer = (d − m) : (m − c)
  • Milk left after n replacements = V(1 − x/V)ⁿ
  • Mean value = (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂)/(n₁ + n₂)
  • Concentration = (quantity of ingredient/total) × 100
  • Ratio after adding water: ingredient stays constant

Step-by-step method

  1. Identify the cheap and costly values and the target mean.
  2. Write the cross diagram and subtract diagonally.
  3. Convert the resulting ratio into quantities.
  4. For replacement, keep the fraction removed each time.

Solved examples

Example 1. In what ratio should rice at ₹40/kg be mixed with rice at ₹60/kg to get a mixture at ₹48/kg?
Solution: Cheaper : Dearer = (60 − 48) : (48 − 40) = 12 : 8 = 3 : 2.
Answer: 3 : 2
Example 2. 20 L of pure milk has 4 L removed and replaced by water. This is done twice. Find the milk left.
Solution: 20 × (1 − 4/20)² = 20 × 0.64 = 12.8 L.
Answer: 12.8 L
Example 3. 50 L of a 20% alcohol solution is given. How much water should be added to make it 10%?
Solution: Alcohol = 10 L. For 10%, total = 100 L. Water to add = 100 − 50 = 50 L.
Answer: 50 L
Example 4. 4 L of a 10% solution is mixed with 6 L of a 20% solution. Find the concentration of the mixture.
Solution: Alcohol = 0.4 + 1.2 = 1.6 L in 10 L = 16%.
Answer: 16%

Shortcuts

  • Equal quantities give the simple average of the two values.
  • The mean always lies between the two prices (closer to the larger quantity).
  • For one replacement with equal quantities, milk left = V(1 − x/V).

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the order of the cross subtraction.
  • Changing the ingredient quantity when adding water.
  • Applying the replacement formula with the wrong volume x.

Ages, Simplification and Approximation

Three small topics that give quick marks: age puzzles, BODMAS simplification and estimating answers when options are far apart.

Start from zero

Age problems

Assign variables to present ages and write one equation for each statement. Remember: after t years, add t to every age. t years ago, subtract t. The difference between two persons' ages never changes. Example: father is 3 times son and the sum is 48, so son = 12, father = 36.

Typical age statements

'Five years ago A was twice B' → A − 5 = 2(B − 5). 'After 10 years the ratio becomes 5 : 4' → (A + 10)/(B + 10) = 5/4. 'The sum of ages is 40' → A + B = 40.
Ages move together on a timeline; the age difference stays fixed

BODMAS

Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division and Multiplication (left to right), Addition and Subtraction (left to right). Example: 18 ÷ 3 × 2 + 5² − 4 = 6 × 2 + 25 − 4 = 33.

Squares, cubes and roots

Memorise squares to 30 and cubes to 15 (see the cheat sheet). Roots of perfect squares then become instant. Example: √1296 = 36.

Approximation

When options are far apart, round numbers to friendly values. Example: 48.7% of 623 ≈ 50% of 620 = 310 (exact 303.4), good enough to choose between 250, 303 and 360.

Formula box

  • Age after t years = present age + t
  • Age difference stays constant
  • BODMAS order: Brackets, Orders, D/M, A/S
  • (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
  • a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)
  • √(a²) = a for a ≥ 0

Step-by-step method

  1. Define variables for present ages.
  2. Write one equation for each sentence.
  3. Solve the equations (substitution or elimination).
  4. Check the answer against the original statements.

Solved examples

Example 1. The sum of the ages of a father and son is 48 years and the father is three times as old as the son. Find their ages.
Solution: Let son = x. Father = 3x. 4x = 48, x = 12. Son 12, father 36.
Answer: Son 12, father 36
Example 2. Five years ago A was twice as old as B. Now their ages sum to 40. Find the present ages.
Solution: A − 5 = 2(B − 5) and A + B = 40. So A = 2B − 5. Then 3B − 5 = 40, B = 15, A = 25.
Answer: A = 25, B = 15
Example 3. Simplify 18 ÷ 3 × 2 + 5² − 4.
Solution: 18 ÷ 3 = 6, 6 × 2 = 12, 5² = 25. 12 + 25 − 4 = 33.
Answer: 33
Example 4. Estimate 48.7% of 623.
Solution: ≈ 50% of 620 = 310; the exact value is 303.4.
Answer: ≈ 303

Shortcuts

  • For ratio age problems, use x as the multiplier.
  • In age sum problems, each year later adds (number of people) to the sum.
  • For approximation, round to the nearest ten or hundred.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to age all persons by the same number of years.
  • Ignoring left-to-right order for division and multiplication.
  • Over-rounding when options are close.

Formula cheat sheet

Every key formula of the section on a few pages. Revise it daily in the last two weeks.

1. Number System

  • Number of factors of N = pᵃqᵇrᶜ → (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)
  • Sum of factors = [(pᵃ⁺¹−1)/(p−1)] × [(qᵇ⁺¹−1)/(q−1)] × …
  • Trailing zeros of n! = ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + …
  • Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder
  • Sum of first n natural numbers = n(n+1)/2
  • Sum of first n odd numbers = n²; first n even numbers = n(n+1)
  • (a+b)² = a²+2ab+b², (a−b)² = a²−2ab+b², a²−b² = (a+b)(a−b)

2. LCM and HCF

  • HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers
  • HCF ≤ every given number ≤ LCM
  • HCF of fractions = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators)
  • LCM of fractions = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators)
  • Smallest number leaving remainder r = LCM + r
  • Greatest number leaving remainder r = HCF(a−r, b−r, …)

3. Percentages

  • x% of N = (x/100) × N
  • Percentage change = (change / original) × 100
  • Successive change = a + b + ab/100
  • If A is x% more than B: B is x/(100+x) × 100 % less than A
  • Population after n years = P (1 + r/100)ⁿ
  • Depreciated value = P (1 − r/100)ⁿ
  • Reduce consumption by r/(100+r) × 100 % when price rises r%

4. Profit and Loss

  • Profit % = (SP − CP)/CP × 100
  • SP = CP × (100 ± P)/100
  • CP = SP × 100/(100 ± P)
  • Discount % = (MP − SP)/MP × 100
  • Successive discount = a + b − ab/100
  • Loss % when equal gain and loss % = x²/100
  • Gain % on false weight = error/(true − error) × 100

5. Time, Speed and Distance

  • Speed = Distance/Time
  • km/h → m/s: × 5/18; m/s → km/h: × 18/5
  • Average speed (equal distances) = 2xy/(x+y)
  • Same direction: relative speed = |u − v|; opposite: u + v
  • Train crossing a platform: (L + P)/speed
  • Two trains: (L₁ + L₂)/relative speed
  • Boat: b = (d+u)/2, s = (d−u)/2

6. Time and Work

  • A and B together = ab/(a+b) days
  • If A alone takes a and A+B take t days, B alone takes at/(a − t)
  • Work = Men × Days × Hours (proportional)
  • Inlet pipe = + rate, outlet pipe = − rate
  • Wages ratio = ratio of (rate × days worked)
  • Total work = LCM of days (efficiency method)

7. Ratio and Proportion

  • a : b = a/b
  • Compound ratio of a:b and c:d = ac : bd
  • If a:b = c:d then ad = bc
  • Mean proportional = √(ab)
  • Third proportional to a, b = b²/a
  • Amount to each = N × (part / total parts)
  • Direct: a₁/b₁ = a₂/b₂; Inverse: a₁b₁ = a₂b₂

8. Averages

  • Average = Sum/Number of items
  • Sum = Average × Number
  • Average of first n natural numbers = (n+1)/2
  • Weighted average = Σ(nᵢaᵢ)/Σnᵢ
  • Change in average on replacing = (new − old)/n
  • New value joining = old average + n × (change in average)

9. Simple and Compound Interest

  • SI = PRT/100
  • A = P + SI
  • A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ (compound)
  • CI = A − P
  • Half-yearly: rate R/2, periods 2n
  • Quarterly: rate R/4, periods 4n
  • CI − SI (2 yrs) = P(R/100)²
  • CI − SI (3 yrs) = P(R/100)²(300 + R)/100

10. Probability
